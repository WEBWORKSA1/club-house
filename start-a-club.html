Launch guide

How to start a club (that people actually stay in)

A practical, step-by-step guide distilled from hundreds of organizer stories, student-activity offices and service-club charter manuals — plus an interactive checklist that saves your progress.

In this guide

  2. Recruit founders
  3. Decide your structure
  4. Money: dues & budget

1. Nail the purpose in one sentence

Every lasting club can explain itself in a single line: "We meet every second Thursday to read and argue about one novel a month." That sentence does three jobs — it tells newcomers whether they belong, tells members what to expect, and tells you what to say no to. If you can't write it yet, you aren't ready to recruit.

Test your sentence on five people who might join. If they ask "so what do you actually do?", sharpen it. Specific beats broad: "beginner trail runners in the east end" outperforms "a running club" because people self-select in.

2. Recruit three to five founding members

A club of one is a hobby. A founding group of three to five people shares the work, brings their networks and makes the first meeting feel alive. Look for people who are reliable before people who are enthusiastic — someone who will show up every time matters more than someone who is excited once.

Give founders a real role from day one: host, secretary (notes and reminders), treasurer (money), and recruiter. Shared ownership is the single strongest predictor of a club surviving its first year.

3. Decide how formal you need to be

Most clubs start informal and add structure as they grow. A simple rule of thumb:

  • Under 15 members, no money: a group chat and a shared calendar are enough.
  • Collecting dues or holding events: write short bylaws, name a treasurer and keep simple records.
  • Signing contracts, renting venues or raising funds publicly: look into registering as a nonprofit association in your region and consider liability insurance.

Our bylaws starter produces a clean first draft covering name, purpose, membership, officers, meetings, finances, amendments and dissolution — the same core articles most student-union and service-club templates require.

4. Money: set dues people don't resent

Dues should cover real costs, not create a profit. List everything for a year — venue, refreshments, software, insurance, event costs — add a 10–15% reserve, then divide by the members you realistically expect to pay. The dues calculator does this for you, including a drop-out allowance.

Keep dues low enough that cost is never the reason someone doesn't join. Fund extras through events and sponsorship instead.

Use a separate account for club money from the start, even if it's a simple payment app sub-account. Mixing club money with personal money is the most common cause of founder burnout and disputes.

5. Pick a venue and a rhythm

Consistency beats convenience. "First Tuesday, 7pm, back room of the library" is easier to remember than a rotating schedule. Free and low-cost options include public libraries, community centres, cafés on quiet nights, parks, places of worship and co-working spaces. For online clubs, choose one platform and stick to it.

Monthly is the most sustainable cadence for discussion clubs; weekly works for activity clubs like running or chess where the activity itself is the draw.

6. Run a first meeting people talk about

  1. Start on time, even if only three people are there.
  2. Do a quick round of names and "why I came".
  3. Explain the purpose sentence and how meetings will work.
  4. Do the actual activity — don't spend the whole meeting on admin.
  5. Agree on the next date before anyone leaves.
  6. Send a short recap within 24 hours with photos and the next date.

Build a timed agenda in seconds with our agenda builder.

7. The first 90 days

New clubs usually lose a third of first-meeting attendees by meeting three. That's normal. Focus on the people who return: ask them what would make it better, give them small jobs, and ask each to bring one friend. After 90 days, review what's working, then grow deliberately using the membership playbook.
